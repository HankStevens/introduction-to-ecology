19  From This Book to Your Résumé: A Skills Crosswalk

…in which we translate what you just did in R into words a hiring manager will recognize–without overselling it.

19.1 Goals

In this appendix, you will

  • See, chapter by chapter, exactly which R tools and quantitative tasks you were exposed to and practiced.
  • Learn the standard industry name for each skill–the words recruiters and applicant-tracking systems actually search for.
  • Get worked examples of turning a chapter assignment into a résumé bullet that is specific and honest about what you actually did.
  • Learn how to talk about this experience credibly in an interview, without claiming more expertise than you have.

19.2 What this course is, and isn’t

This is an ecology course, not a data-science bootcamp, and this appendix is not asking you to pretend otherwise. You are not being trained as a professional data analyst. What you are getting, most chapters, is real exposure to what practicing ecologists actually do with data and models: querying a live database, fitting a model, making a forecast, building a figure–using R code that is mostly provided to you, which you run, adapt in small ways, and interpret.

That is genuinely useful, real experience. It is not the same thing as designing an analysis from scratch or understanding the statistical theory behind it. Running a Generalized Additive Mixed Model (GAMM) and correctly reading its forecast is not the same as understanding why a GAMM works the way it does–that second thing is graduate-level material. Both are true at once: you did something real and specific, and you should not overstate what it means.

The rest of this appendix is about naming what you did accurately–specific enough to be credible, honest enough to survive a follow-up question in an interview.

19.3 The core skills you’re already building

Two things recur across nearly every chapter, as the Preface mentions: wrangling data with tidyverse-style functions (read_csv, subset, mutate, and similar), and visualizing it with ggplot2. By the time you finish this book you will have done both dozens of times, on dozens of different real datasets. That repetition–not any single chapter–is what actually makes those two skills worth naming: you can honestly say you’ve practiced them across a whole semester of varied, real data.

The table below focuses on what’s different about each chapter, on top of that recurring foundation. A small number of chapters (noted in the table) don’t use ggplot2 or tidyverse wrangling at all.

19.4 How to use this appendix, honestly

For each chapter you complete, find its row below. The distinctive skill column names what made that chapter different–in addition to the recurring wrangling/visualization work above.

When you turn any of this into a résumé bullet or interview answer, favor verbs that match what you actually did:

  • Honest: used, ran, practiced, interpreted, worked with, applied [a provided method].
  • Overclaiming, unless you genuinely wrote the method yourself rather than adapting provided code: built, engineered, developed, designed.

You do not need to list all 19 chapters on a résumé. Pick 3-5 that showcase different kinds of tasks (for example: one API/live-data chapter, one modeling/simulation chapter, one statistics chapter), and write one honest, specific bullet for each rather than a long, generic list.

19.6 Which chapters best showcase which kind of exposure

If you want to lead with a specific kind of experience on a résumé, these are your strongest, most concrete pieces of evidence:

Live API integration / working with real-world data feeds – Funding Ecological Science (USAspending.gov, NSF Award Search), GBIF Biodiversity Data (rgbif), Traits and Physiology (BIEN). These are the most immediately recognizable to a non-academic employer: you queried a live government or scientific database, not a cleaned CSV someone handed you.

Data wrangling and reshaping (“tidy data”) – Funding Ecological Science (joining agency budgets, CPI, and state apportionment data from different sources), Traits and Physiology (long-to-wide reshaping), ForestGEO/GBIF (standardizing across sites).

Exposure to statistical modeling – Global Temperature Anomalies (GAMM/time-series forecasting), Extra-Pair Copulation (logistic regression), Metabolic Ecology (multiple regression and diagnostics). “Exposure to” is the honest frame here–you ran and interpreted these models; you were not asked to derive or fully understand the underlying theory.

Exposure to simulation and dynamical/mathematical modeling – Density-Dependence, Predator-Prey, and Carbon Cycle chapters (differential-equation modeling with deSolve); Structured Demography (matrix/eigenanalysis).

Data visualization – present in most chapters (see the core-skills note above), but Global Temperature Anomalies, Predator-Prey, and Thinking about Concepts push furthest into multi-panel, publication-style ggplot2 figures.

Comfortable working outside ggplot2/tidyverse – Structured Demography and Direct Species Interactions use base R graphics and linear algebra instead. Worth mentioning too: it shows you’re not tied to one toolkit.

19.7 Worked examples: chapter to résumé bullet

Each of these names the real tool and the real task, without claiming you designed the method yourself:

Funding Ecological Science → “Used R (jsonlite, tidyverse) to query live federal budget APIs (USAspending.gov, NSF Award Search) and join them with inflation and state-apportionment data, producing agency-by-agency funding comparisons.”

GBIF Biodiversity Data → “Used R (rgbif) to query a live biodiversity database (GBIF), assembling species-occurrence data across a latitudinal gradient and applying provided rarefaction and regression methods to examine a sampling-effort question.”

Predator-Prey (Isle Royale) → “Used a provided differential-equation model (deSolve, R) to fit 60+ years of empirical wolf-moose population data, and evaluated how well a classical ecological theory (Lotka-Volterra predator-prey dynamics) matched the real-world pattern.”

Structured Demography (Loggerhead Turtles) → “Used a provided stage-structured matrix population model (eigenanalysis, R) to identify which life stages most influence loggerhead sea turtle population growth, and interpreted the results for a conservation-management question.”

Global Temperature Anomalies → “Used a provided generalized additive mixed model, or GAMM (mgcv, R) to forecast global temperature trends with quantified uncertainty, and communicated the results through a publication-style figure.”

Compare these to a version that oversells: “Built a forecasting engine” or “developed a predictive model” implies you designed the method. “Used a provided model to…” is still specific and still names real tools and real tasks–it just doesn’t claim ownership you don’t have.

19.8 Being honest about what you know

A few rules of thumb for staying credible, especially in an interview:

  • If someone asks a follow-up question you can’t answer–“why a GAMM instead of a linear model?”, “how does the eigenanalysis actually work?”–the honest answer is some version of: “That was the method used in the course. I understand what it produced and why it mattered for the question we were asking, but I haven’t studied the underlying theory in depth.” That is a completely normal, credible thing for an undergraduate to say, and it holds up far better than getting caught overselling.
  • What you can honestly claim is broader than any single technique: a semester of hands-on, repeated practice reading, cleaning, visualizing, and interpreting real data, across many different real-world sources and problems. That pattern–not mastery of any one method–is the actual transferable skill, and it’s a genuinely useful thing to be able to say.

19.9 Building a portfolio, not just a bullet list

A résumé line is words on a page; a public, working example is proof–and it’s also a natural way to show, rather than claim, the level of understanding you actually have. Your regular assignments in this course are turned in as a Word document plus a separate R script–a format that works well for grading, but isn’t something you can link to as a live, running example.

This book itself is written entirely in Quarto–a tool that combines code, narrative text, and output (figures, tables) into a single, reproducible, publishable document. You have not been asked to use Quarto for your regular assignments, and that’s a reasonable choice for a first ecology course. But if you want a portfolio piece, learning just enough Quarto to rebuild 1-2 of your best analyses as short documents–with your own explanation, in your own words, of what the code does and what you do and don’t understand about it–is a real, additional skill, and a more honest way to demonstrate your actual level of understanding than a résumé bullet alone:

  1. Pick 1-2 analyses you understood well enough to explain to someone else.
  2. Rebuild each as a short Quarto (.qmd) document combining your code, your figure, and your own written explanation–including, where relevant, an honest note about what you’re still fuzzy on.
  3. Render them as a small Quarto website (a few lines of _quarto.yml configuration) and publish it for free (e.g., GitHub Pages, Quarto Pub).
  4. Link that site from your résumé and LinkedIn profile instead of–or alongside–the bullet points above.

19.10 Quick-reference skill glossary

Pair any of these with an honest verb (used, practiced, interpreted, worked with) rather than an ownership verb (built, engineered, developed), unless you genuinely wrote the method yourself:

  • REST API integration / live data querying
  • JSON parsing
  • Data wrangling / data cleaning
  • Tidy data / data reshaping
  • Exposure to statistical modeling (regression, GAMM, logistic regression)
  • Exposure to differential equation modeling / dynamical systems simulation
  • Exposure to matrix modeling / linear algebra (eigenanalysis)
  • Bayesian statistical reasoning
  • Causal inference / causal diagrams (DAGs)
  • Data visualization (ggplot2, publication-quality graphics)
  • Reproducible research / literate programming (Quarto)–only if you build the optional portfolio site above; regular assignments don’t currently provide this
  • Version control (if you use git/GitHub for your assignments)
