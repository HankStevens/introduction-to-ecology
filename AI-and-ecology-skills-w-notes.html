AI and the Changing Landscape of Skills in Ecology

What should you learn, when the machine already “knows” so much?

Background

You are learning ecology at a moment when artificial intelligence can write passable R code, summarize journal articles, and define terms like “carrying capacity” faster than you can open a textbook. That fact changes what it means to be well-trained in ecology. It does not, however, reduce the amount you need to learn — it shifts what you need to learn.

This chapter is addressed to both students and instructors. Its goal is to lay out a practical framework for thinking about which skills matter more than ever in an era of large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT, Claude, and their successors, and which tasks can be responsibly shared with these tools. We focus on quantitative ecology because that is what this book is about, but the ideas extend broadly.

The central argument is this: AI’s fluency with ecological language makes it more important, not less, that you develop deep understanding of ecological mechanisms, model assumptions, and data interpretation. A machine can generate a plausible-sounding paragraph about density dependence in thirty seconds. Only a well-trained ecologist can recognize when that paragraph is subtly wrong.

A framework: durable skills vs. AI-augmentable tasks

Not all skills are equally vulnerable to automation. A useful way to think about your education is to distinguish between two categories.

Durable human skills are things that require biological intuition, judgment under uncertainty, and the ability to connect mathematical abstractions to the living world. These are hard-won, slow to develop, and not reliably performed by current AI. They include:

  • Ecological intuition — the ability to walk into a forest, a marsh, or a dataset and notice what is ecologically interesting or surprising.
  • Mechanistic reasoning — understanding why a model behaves the way it does, not just that it produces a particular output.
  • Model assumption literacy — knowing what a model assumes, when those assumptions are reasonable, and what happens when they are violated.
  • Data literacy — the judgment to know whether data are adequate to answer a question, what biases they carry, and what they cannot tell you.
  • Multi-scale synthesis — connecting processes at the scale of an individual organism to patterns at the scale of a landscape or a globe.

AI-augmentable tasks are things that AI tools can do reasonably well right now, and will likely do better over time. These include:

  • Summarizing the main findings of a journal article.
  • Generating boilerplate R code for standard analyses (e.g., fitting a linear model, making a scatterplot).
  • Looking up definitions of ecological terms.
  • Drafting outlines or initial structure for a written argument.
  • Translating between programming languages.

Here is the key insight: AI-augmentable does not mean unimportant. You still need to understand what a linear model does and why, even if you let an AI draft the code. The danger is not that AI does these tasks — it is that you might skip the understanding that makes you capable of evaluating whether the AI did them correctly.

Why AI makes ecological understanding more important

This point deserves emphasis, because it is counterintuitive. You might think: if AI can write my R code and summarize my readings, I can spend less time on those things and more time on… something else. But consider what happens in practice.

Suppose you ask an AI to write R code that simulates logistic population growth. The AI will almost certainly produce something that runs. But will it use the continuous-time or discrete-time model? Will it handle the time lag that makes the discrete model capable of oscillations and chaos? Will it set up the parameters in a biologically meaningful range? If you do not know enough to answer these questions, you cannot evaluate the code. You are flying blind.

Or suppose you ask an AI to explain why two competing species cannot coexist indefinitely. The AI will probably give you something that sounds like the competitive exclusion principle. But it might gloss over the specific conditions required, or it might fail to mention that the principle applies to a particular model with particular assumptions, and that real communities are far more complicated. The explanation will sound right. Whether it is right depends on what you, the human ecologist, know.

ImportantThe Plausibility Trap

AI-generated text about ecology is often plausible but wrong in ways that require ecological training to detect. The better the AI gets at sounding like an ecologist, the more you need to actually be one.

This is what we call the plausibility trap. AI systems are trained to produce text (and code) that looks like text (and code) produced by experts. They are not trained to be correct about the natural world. They have no field experience, no memory of watching a population crash, no intuition about whether a parameter value makes biological sense. They are pattern-matching engines — extraordinarily powerful ones, but ones that lack the grounding in physical and biological reality that you are here to develop.

Six categories of skills to develop

Let us get specific. Below are five categories of durable skills, each illustrated with examples from quantitative ecology. These are the skills this textbook is designed to build, and the ones you should invest in most heavily.

1. Mechanistic reasoning

Mechanistic reasoning is the ability to explain why an ecological pattern exists by tracing it back to underlying biological processes. It is the difference between saying “the population oscillates” and saying “the population oscillates because the discrete-time logistic model builds in a one-generation time lag between when crowding occurs and when its effects on reproduction are realized, and when \(r\) is large enough, this lag causes the population to overshoot and undershoot \(K\) repeatedly.”

AI can describe patterns. It can even parrot mechanistic explanations it has seen in training data. But it cannot generate reliable mechanistic reasoning about novel situations — situations where you are looking at a dataset or a model output that nobody has analyzed before. That is what ecologists do.

How to practice it. Throughout this book, when you are asked to run a simulation or examine data, always ask yourself: Why does this pattern exist? What mechanism produces it? Do not settle for describing what you see. Explain what causes it.

2. Model assumption literacy

Every ecological model is a set of assumptions about how the world works. The exponential growth model assumes unlimited resources and no density dependence. The logistic model assumes that per capita growth rate declines linearly with population size. The Lotka-Volterra competition model assumes that the effect of one competitor on another is constant regardless of density.

These assumptions are not fine print. They are the substance of the model. When an assumption is violated, the model’s predictions can be wildly wrong. Understanding which assumptions matter and when they might be violated is one of the most important skills in ecology.

TipA Useful Habit

Before running any model, write down its key assumptions in your own words. Then ask: Which of these assumptions could be wrong for the system I am studying, and how would that change the predictions?

AI tools are particularly poor at evaluating whether a model’s assumptions are met in a specific real-world situation. They can list assumptions from a textbook, but they cannot walk outside and check whether density dependence in your study population really is linear, or whether the competitive effect of species A on species B really is constant. That judgment requires you.

3. Prediction before computation

One of the most effective learning strategies in quantitative ecology is to make a prediction before you run the code. What do you think will happen when you double the intrinsic growth rate? What do you expect the graph to look like when you increase carrying capacity? What will happen to coexistence if you make one species a much stronger competitor?

If you write down your prediction and it turns out to be wrong, you have learned something valuable: there is a gap between your mental model and the mathematical model. That gap is where real understanding develops.

AI eliminates this step if you let it. You can ask an AI what will happen, and it will tell you, and you will nod and move on without ever experiencing the productive discomfort of being wrong. Resist this. Your mistakes are the raw material of learning.

How to practice it. Every time this textbook asks you to run a simulation or modify a parameter, pause. Write down, in one sentence, what you predict will happen and why. Then run the code and compare. If you were wrong, figure out why.

4. Data literacy and judgment

Data literacy goes beyond knowing how to make a scatterplot or compute a mean. It includes:

  • Recognizing what kinds of variation are present in a dataset (measurement error, natural variation, sampling bias).
  • Understanding what a dataset cannot tell you because of how it was collected.
  • Knowing when a sample size is too small to support a conclusion.
  • Distinguishing correlation from causation with genuine understanding rather than just reciting the phrase.
  • Recognizing when an apparent pattern might be an artifact of how the data were processed.

These judgments are context-dependent and require understanding of both the statistical tools and the biological system. AI can compute a p-value, but it cannot tell you whether the study design that produced the data makes that p-value meaningful.

How to practice it. When you encounter data in this book, do not just analyze them. Interrogate them. How were they collected? What could go wrong? What alternative explanations exist for the patterns you see?

5. Multi-scale synthesis

Ecology spans scales from molecules to the biosphere. A well-trained ecologist can connect processes at different scales: how individual behavior gives rise to population dynamics, how population dynamics shape community composition, how community composition affects ecosystem function.

This kind of synthesis requires holding multiple frameworks in your head simultaneously and recognizing when processes at one scale constrain or enable processes at another. AI can retrieve information about any one of these scales, but it struggles to make the cross-scale connections that produce genuine ecological insight.

How to practice it. As you move through this book from individual ecology to population dynamics to community ecology to ecosystems, keep asking: How does what I learned in earlier chapters connect to what I am learning now?

Learn to distinguish between understanding and doing

You will need to distinguish between code that you check primarily with the output vs. code you check primarily via assumptions.

Code that generates data displays, apps, or games is code that you check primarily via output. You look at or use the output and ask, did the code do what I wanted it to do? If not, attempt to troubleshoot and try again.

In contrast, code that generates mechanistic and statistical models is code that you have to understand and check primarily by evaluating assumptions that you tried to manifest in your code.

Mechanistic models are simply tools to turn assumptions into predictions, so you really need to understand how you are building those assumptions.

The output of your mechanistic model constitutes your predictions. You need to trust those predictions because they follow directly from the assumptions that you made. If the predictions seem off to you, then you need to reconsider the assumptions you made. There are three types of situations to consider:

  1. Do you have a simple coding error? Does your code do what you intended? E.g., did you use Boolean logic correctly, or create input to a function that merely wasn’t in the right sequence?
  2. Does your math match your ecology? How you use mathematical functions to manifest your assumptions about the biological/ecological/physical features of the world can be tricky and problems fall into two buckets: sometimes the math doesn’t do what you think it does, and other times the ecological assumptions have different consequences than you thought they would.
    1. Are your mathematical assumptions correct? There are some convenient mathematical operations and forms that you can collect in your toolbox: functions that put a ceiling or floor on values, functions that create positive feedback loops and negative feedback loops, functions that add and those that subtract; 0th, 1st, and 2nd order products of variables.
    2. Are your ecological assumptions correct? This is the interesting situation, where your choices of mathematical functions or parameter estimates may or may not map onto reality.
      1. If your ecological assumptions are wrong, that is great! You’re learning something important about the world.
      2. If your ecological assumptions are correct, but the outcome is unexpected, that is great - you are learning something!

How to practice it. When you encounter a place in this book where you need to use code, do not just copy some code nor ask AI for it. Ask why am I writing code?

  1. Is it to make a pretty graph of data to display results to assess my predictions and hypothesis?
  2. Is it to use mathematics to represent some biological or physical process?
  3. Is it to perform a statistical test in which I have to understand the assumptions of that test to apply it correctly?

If you answer “yes” to no. 1, then using AI is relatively safe, but you can probably be most effective if you can work directly with any code AI spits out for you.

If you answer “yes” to 2 or 3 above, then you really need to understand what you are doing. You need to know not only that two cans of beer plus two cans of beer add up to four cans of beer, but what that beer can do once you have it.

Using AI responsibly in this course

None of the above means you should avoid AI entirely. AI tools can be genuinely useful in your ecological education, but they are useful in the way a calculator is useful in a math course: they can save time on mechanical tasks, but they cannot replace the thinking that makes the tasks meaningful.

Here are some guidelines for responsible use.

Good uses of AI in ecology coursework:

  • Debugging R code that throws an error you do not understand.
  • Getting a quick refresher on R syntax (e.g., “How do I add a legend to a ggplot?”).
  • Generating a first draft of an outline that you then revise substantially.
  • Asking for alternative explanations of a concept you have already studied but find confusing.

Risky uses of AI in ecology coursework:

  • Asking AI to explain an ecological concept you have not yet studied yourself. (You will not be able to evaluate the answer.)
  • Using AI-generated code without understanding every line of it.
  • Asking AI to interpret your results for you. (Interpretation requires the biological context that you, not the AI, possess.)
  • Asking AI to write your discussion or analysis. (This is where your own ecological thinking is supposed to develop.)
WarningThe Code Comprehension Rule

If you use AI to help write R code, you should be able to explain what every line does and why it is there. If you cannot, you have not learned what you need to learn.

A note for instructors

If you are teaching with this book, the AI landscape is shifting under your feet. Here are some principles that may help.

Design assignments that require judgment, not just output. If an assignment can be completed by pasting the prompt into ChatGPT and submitting the result, the assignment is testing the wrong thing. Assignments should require students to make predictions, evaluate assumptions, connect models to specific biological systems, and explain why a result makes ecological sense. These are tasks that require the durable skills described above.

Make AI use transparent, not forbidden. Blanket bans on AI are difficult to enforce and send the message that AI is cheating rather than a tool. Instead, consider requiring students to document when and how they used AI, and to reflect on what they learned (or failed to learn) by using it. The goal is to develop students who can use AI wisely, not students who can pretend they do not use it.

Foreground assumptions explicitly. When teaching any model, spend time on what the model assumes and what would happen if those assumptions were violated. Use callout boxes, discussion questions, and activities that force students to engage with assumptions as substantive ecological content, not as disclaimers to be skipped.

Require prediction before computation. Build the “predict first” step into your workflow explicitly. Have students write predictions in a worksheet, a shared document, or their lab notebooks before they run code. This simple intervention creates the productive struggle that drives learning.

Use AI failures as teaching moments. Ask an AI to explain an ecological concept, and then have students identify what it got wrong. This is an excellent exercise in critical thinking and assumption literacy. It also demonstrates, concretely, why human expertise matters.

The skills that will define your career

The ecology profession is changing. Ecologists who graduate in the next decade will work alongside AI tools that can process satellite imagery, classify species from audio recordings, and generate code for complex statistical analyses. The value of a human ecologist will not lie in being able to do these things manually. It will lie in being able to:

  • Ask the right questions about the natural world.
  • Design studies and models that can actually answer those questions.
  • Recognize when a tool (AI or otherwise) is giving you the wrong answer.
  • Connect mathematical results to biological reality.
  • Communicate ecological understanding to people who need it — policymakers, land managers, communities.

These are the skills this book is designed to develop. The AI tools will change. The need for ecologists who understand the living world will not.

Deliverables

  1. Reflection (individual, ~300 words). Choose one of the five durable skill categories (mechanistic reasoning, model assumption literacy, prediction before computation, data literacy, or multi-scale synthesis). Describe a specific moment in your education — in this course or another — when you were forced to use this skill. What made it difficult? What did you learn? How would the experience have been different if you had used AI to skip the hard part?

  2. AI evaluation exercise (pairs). Pick an ecological concept covered in a previous chapter of this book (e.g., exponential growth, logistic growth, competitive exclusion, density dependence). Ask an AI to explain this concept in 2–3 paragraphs. Then, working with your partner, write a critical evaluation of the AI’s response:

    1. What did the AI get right?
    2. What did it get wrong, or what important nuance did it miss?
    3. What assumptions or caveats were absent from the AI’s explanation?
    4. Would a student who read only the AI’s response develop a correct understanding of the concept? Why or why not?

  3. Assumption audit (individual). Choose one model from this course (e.g., the discrete-time logistic model, the Lotka-Volterra competition model). List its key assumptions. For each assumption, write one sentence explaining a real ecological situation in which that assumption would be violated, and one sentence explaining what consequence the violation would have for the model’s predictions. Do not use AI for this exercise.
